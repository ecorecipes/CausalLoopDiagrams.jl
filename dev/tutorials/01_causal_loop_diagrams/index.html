Overview

A causal loop diagram is the first thing drawn when a group of people describe a system: factors joined by arrows, a sign on each arrow, loops closing back on themselves. Baez formalises one as a graph whose edges are labelled by elements of a monoid of polarities, which makes a labelling the same thing as a functor from the free category on the graph into the delooping of that monoid (Baez 2024); with Chaudhuri he goes on to three kinds of morphism between labelled graphs – refining, simplifying, finding motifs – and to a homology that counts the feedback a composite acquires that neither part had (Baez and Chaudhuri 2025).

A Bayesian network carries much more than a sign per arrow, and one thing less: it must be acyclic, so it cannot hold a loop at all. This vignette walks the translation in both directions and is as interested in what it loses as in what it keeps.

using CausalLoopDiagrams
using BayesianNetworks

Four polarities, and why not three

The basic case is the sign group, {+, -}. Adding an element for “indeterminate” gives Baez’s three-element monoid {+, 0, -}. This package uses four, because a conditional probability table distinguishes two things that monoid writes alike:

polarities()
(0, +, -, ?)

NO_EFFECT is the link along which nothing moves – a table that does not depend on that parent at all – and INDETERMINATE is a link whose direction the diagram does not determine. On a whiteboard the two are indistinguishable; in a table they are different facts, and the first is a finding rather than an absence of one.

The four values are the subsets {0}, {+}, {-} and {0, +, -} of the sign hyperfield, and those four subsets are closed under its multiplication and its (multivalued) addition. So they form a commutative rig: a sub-rig of the power-set rig Baez builds from the sign hyperfield. Multiplication composes links along a path and addition combines links in parallel.

println("composition along a path:")
for a in polarities()
    println("  ", [a * b for b in polarities()])
end
println("links in parallel:")
for a in polarities()
    println("  ", [a + b for b in polarities()])
end
println("units: one = ", one(Polarity), ", zero = ", zero(Polarity))
composition along a path:
  Polarity[0, 0, 0, 0]
  Polarity[0, +, -, ?]
  Polarity[0, -, +, ?]
  Polarity[0, ?, ?, ?]
links in parallel:
  Polarity[0, +, -, ?]
  Polarity[+, +, ?, ?]
  Polarity[-, ?, -, ?]
  Polarity[?, ?, ?, ?]
units: one = +, zero = 0

Two things to read off those tables. NO_EFFECT is absorbing for composition – a path through a dead link is dead – and the unit for addition. And a positive link beside a negative one adds to ?: two pathways that disagree, with nothing in the formalism to say which wins. That is not a gap in the implementation, it is what a sign is.

A sketch, and its loops

Here is a seasonal tree disease as a practitioner would sketch it. Inoculum meets host tissue and infection follows; infection replenishes the inoculum for next season and damages the host; and a damaged host carries less tissue to infect. delayed marks the links that do not act within the period.

sketch = causal_loop_diagram([(:Inoculum, :Infection) => POSITIVE,
                              (:Infection, :Damage) => POSITIVE,
                              (:Damage, :Tissue) => NEGATIVE,
                              delayed((:Tissue, :Infection) => POSITIVE),
                              delayed((:Infection, :Inoculum) => POSITIVE)])
draw(sketch, "sketch.png")

foreach(println, loops(sketch))
loop_summary(sketch)
Inoculum -> Infection -> Inoculum (+, reinforcing, delayed)
Infection -> Damage -> Tissue -> Infection (-, balancing, delayed)

(loops = 2, reinforcing = 1, balancing = 1, undetermined = 0, inactive = 0, delayed = 2, instantaneous = 0)

Two loops that pull against each other. The inoculum loop is reinforcing: a heavy season leaves a heavy inoculum. The damage loop is balancing: an epidemic that defoliates its host removes what it needs. A loop’s polarity is the product of the signs around it, and because the rig is commutative it does not depend on where the loop is cut –

(loop_polarity(sketch, [:Infection, :Damage, :Tissue]),
 loop_polarity(sketch, [:Tissue, :Infection, :Damage]))
(-, -)

– which is what makes it a property of the loop rather than of how it was written down.

Reading a sign off a table

Nothing so far has needed probabilities. The bridge to the networks is polarity, which reads a sign off a conditional probability table by stochastic monotonicity in the declared order of the states: the child’s distribution rises with the parent if its upper tail never shrinks, for every configuration of the other parents.

rain = FiniteAxis(:Rain, [:dry, :wet])
grass = FiniteAxis(:Grass, [:brown, :green])

(polarity(cpt(rain, grass, [0.9 0.1; 0.2 0.8]), :Rain),
 polarity(cpt(rain, grass, [0.1 0.9; 0.8 0.2]), :Rain),
 polarity(cpt(rain, grass, [0.5 0.5; 0.5 0.5]), :Rain))
(+, -, 0)

Dominance is a stronger requirement than a rising mean, and deliberately so. The table below has damage increasing in load on average, but the middle row does not dominate the first – less chance of none and less chance of severe – so there is no one direction to report:

load = FiniteAxis(:Load, [:low, :medium, :high])
damage = FiniteAxis(:Damage, [:none, :some, :severe])
spread = cpt(load, damage, [0.6 0.1 0.3; 0.2 0.7 0.1; 0.1 0.2 0.7])
polarity(spread, :Load)
?

An interaction between two parents comes out the same way, which is the honest answer: the sign of one link depends on the state of another factor, and a label on an edge has nowhere to put that.

allele = FiniteAxis(:Allele, [:absent, :present])
exposure = FiniteAxis(:Exposure, [:low, :high])
harm = FiniteAxis(:Harm, [:no, :yes])
table = Array{Float64}(undef, 2, 2, 2)
table[1, 1, :] = [0.8, 0.2]      # no allele, low exposure
table[1, 2, :] = [0.4, 0.6]      # no allele, high exposure: worse
table[2, 1, :] = [0.9, 0.1]      # allele, low exposure
table[2, 2, :] = [0.95, 0.05]    # allele, high exposure: better
interacting = cpt([allele, exposure], harm, table)

(polarity(interacting, :Exposure), polarity(interacting, :Allele))
(?, -)

Why this is a labelling and not a convention

Monotone kernels compose. If the child’s distribution rises with the parent and a further kernel is monotone in the child, then the composite rises with the parent – so the polarity of a path really is the product of the polarities of its links, which is exactly what Baez’s lemma requires of a labelling. It is worth checking rather than asserting:

sheep = FiniteAxis(:Sheep, [:few, :many])
first_links = [cpt(rain, grass, [0.9 0.1; 0.2 0.8]), cpt(rain, grass, [0.1 0.9; 0.8 0.2])]
second_links = [cpt(grass, sheep, [0.7 0.3; 0.25 0.75]),
                cpt(grass, sheep, [0.3 0.7; 0.75 0.25])]

for f in first_links, g in second_links
    composite = polarity(compose_kernel(f, g), :Rain)
    product = polarity(f, :Rain) * polarity(g, :Grass)
    println(polarity(f, :Rain), " then ", polarity(g, :Grass), " gives ", composite,
            "; the product is ", product, "  (",
            composite === product ? "agree" : "DISAGREE", ")")
end
+ then + gives +; the product is +  (agree)
+ then - gives -; the product is -  (agree)
- then + gives -; the product is -  (agree)
- then - gives +; the product is +  (agree)

Two readings of one network

A causal loop diagram’s arrow claims causation, but diagrams get drawn in workshops from things that were seen to move together. Those are different questions, and causal_loop_diagram will answer either one about a model whose tables are bound.

The reference habitat network is a tree with no confounding in it, and the two readings agree on every link:

habitat = reference_habitat_model()
structural = causal_loop_diagram(habitat)
observational = causal_loop_diagram(habitat; mode = :observational)
draw(structural, "habitat.png")
compare_diagrams(structural, observational)
DiagramComparison: 6 agree, 0 disagree, 0 only in the first, 0 only in the second

<img src="01causalloopdiagramsfigures/habitat.png" style="width:85.0%" />

Figure 2: The reference habitat network read as a causal loop diagram. It is acyclic, as every Bayesian network must be, so it has no feedback in it.

Now a model with confounding. Fertiliser goes on poor soil; soil drives yield harder than fertiliser does. Both mechanisms are monotone, so neither link is indeterminate:

bn = bayesnet(:Soil => [:poor, :rich], :Fertiliser => [:none, :some],
              :Yield => [:low, :high];
              mechanisms = [:Fertiliser => :Soil, :Yield => (:Soil, :Fertiliser)])
yield = Array{Float64}(undef, 2, 2, 2)
yield[1, 1, :] = [0.9, 0.1]      # poor soil, no fertiliser
yield[1, 2, :] = [0.7, 0.3]      # poor soil, fertiliser
yield[2, 1, :] = [0.3, 0.7]      # rich soil, no fertiliser
yield[2, 2, :] = [0.1, 0.9]      # rich soil, fertiliser
farm = bind_cpt(BayesModel(bn),
                [:Soil => [0.5, 0.5], :Fertiliser => [0.1 0.9; 0.9 0.1],
                 :Yield => yield])

compare_diagrams(causal_loop_diagram(farm),
                 causal_loop_diagram(farm; mode = :observational))
DiagramComparison: 2 agree, 1 disagree, 0 only in the first, 0 only in the second
  Fertiliser -> Yield: + against -

Applying fertiliser raises the yield and observing it lowers the expectation, because fertiliser marks poor soil. A diagram drawn from co-movement has that link signed backwards while claiming to be causal, and the disagreement list is the check – computed, not argued.

Where the loops come back

The sketch had two loops; the networks above have none, because that is what acyclicity means. The loops are not lost, though: in a dynamic network they are carried by the lags. A transition template is an ordinary acyclic network over variables named X and X[t-1], and quotienting the time index puts the feedback back.

grazing = vegetation_herbivore_model()
template = causal_loop_diagram(transition_network(grazing))
draw(template, "template.png")
(nfactors = nfactors(template), loops = length(loops(template)))
(nfactors = 4, loops = 0)

<img src="01causalloopdiagramsfigures/template.png" style="width:85.0%" />

Figure 3: The transition template as a causal loop diagram, with the lagged variables still separate factors. Acyclic, so no loops.

collapsed = causal_loop_diagram(grazing)
draw(collapsed, "collapsed.png")
foreach(println, loops(collapsed))
loop_summary(collapsed)
Vegetation -> Vegetation (+, reinforcing, delayed)
Vegetation -> Herbivores -> Vegetation (-, balancing, delayed)

(loops = 2, reinforcing = 1, balancing = 1, undetermined = 0, inactive = 0, delayed = 2, instantaneous = 0)

<img src="01causalloopdiagramsfigures/collapsed.png" style="width:55.0%" />

Figure 4: The same template with the time index collapsed. The loops are back, and the links that crossed a slice boundary are the dashed ones.

Grazing comes out balancing – more vegetation, more herbivores, less vegetation next season – and the sign was computed from the conditional probability tables rather than asserted. Vegetation persisting from one slice to the next comes back as a self-link, which is the shortest feedback loop there is.

The two kinds of loop

That distinction is the one that decides what can be modelled, and it is why a link carries a delay flag at all.

mixed = causal_loop_diagram([(:Price, :Supply) => POSITIVE,
                             (:Supply, :Price) => NEGATIVE,
                             (:Stock, :Harvest) => POSITIVE,
                             delayed((:Harvest, :Stock) => NEGATIVE)])
println("delayed:       ", delayed_loops(mixed))
println("instantaneous: ", instantaneous_loops(mixed))
delayed:       Loop[Stock -> Harvest -> Stock (-, balancing, delayed)]
instantaneous: Loop[Price -> Supply -> Price (-, balancing, instantaneous)]

A delayed loop is one a dynamic Bayesian network holds as it stands. An instantaneous loop asserts that everything in it happens at once; no Bayesian network holds one, and unrolling does not help, because there is no slice boundary for the closing link to cross. Representing one means changing the model – splitting the factor into stages, or admitting a delay the sketch left out.

Holding a sketch against a model

The last use is the one that keeps a modelling exercise honest. Put the sketch beside the diagram read off the finished network, and the differences are a list of what the modelling did.

one_season = causal_loop_diagram([(:Inoculum, :Infection) => POSITIVE,
                                  (:Infection, :Damage) => POSITIVE,
                                  (:Damage, :Tissue) => POSITIVE,
                                  (:Weather, :Infection) => POSITIVE])
compare_diagrams(sketch, one_season)
DiagramComparison: 2 agree, 1 disagree, 2 only in the first, 1 only in the second
  Damage -> Tissue: - against +
  Tissue -(+)-> Infection: only in the first
  Infection -(+)-> Inoculum: only in the first
  Weather -(+)-> Infection: only in the second

Three kinds of entry, and each is worth a question. A link only in the sketch is feedback the network cut. A link only in the network is something the sketch had not thought of. A link both have with different signs is a disagreement that one of the two has got wrong.

Coarsening, and the arithmetic it needs

Baez’s simplifying morphism is the push-forward of edge labels along a map of graphs, and it is the operation that forces the labels to be addable: when two factors merge, the links between them and a third become parallel and have to be combined.

pathways = causal_loop_diagram([(:Shade, :Growth) => NEGATIVE,
                                (:Shelter, :Growth) => POSITIVE])
canopy = coarsen(pathways, Dict(:Shade => :Canopy, :Shelter => :Canopy))
canopy

CausalLoopDiagrams.CausalLoopDiagram {Factor:2, Link:1, Label:0, Sign:0, Flag:0}

Linklink_sourcelink_targetlink_polaritylink_delay

Two pathways of opposite sign become one link labelled ?. That is the right answer and a lossy one: the coarse diagram cannot say whether a canopy helps, and the fine one could not say which effect was bigger either. Merging two factors that were linked to each other turns that link into a self-link, which is a feedback loop of length one – not a defect of the operation, but what coarsening does to the detail inside a factor.

What a polarity cannot carry

Worth stating plainly, because it is the reason a sketch is a sketch and a network is a model.

  • No magnitude. A diagram cannot say which of two loops through one factor wins. That is why Baez’s series moves on from monoids to rings, hyperfields and rigs with more in them than signs.
  • No interaction. A link whose direction depends on another factor comes out ?, and a lever worth more alongside an intervention than alone has no expression in a diagram at all.
  • No unordered variable. “Rises” needs an order on the states. A platform choice, a delivery route, a species identity has none, and causal_loop_diagram takes an unordered keyword so that the links touching one are reported as undetermined rather than guessed:
guessing = causal_loop_diagram(habitat; unordered = [:Vegetation])
(link_polarity(guessing, :SoilMoisture, :Vegetation),
 link_polarity(guessing, :Vegetation, :HabitatQuality),
 link_polarity(guessing, :Climate, :SoilMoisture))
(?, ?, +)

A diagram over a decision between incomparable options has very little to say, and saying so is the useful part.
